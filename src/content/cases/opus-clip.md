The founders of OpusClip did not set out to build a clipping tool. They built livestreaming tools, and those tools did not take off. What users kept asking for was one small feature: pulling the highlights out of long streams. The team listened, rebuilt the company around that feature, and by March 2025 it was reported to be at about $20M in annual recurring revenue ([unverified, ARR Club](https://www.arr.club/signal/opusclip-20m-arr-softbank-mar-2025)). The failed product was the research that found the successful one.

## What made OpusClip's founders leave livestreaming?

The team first built livestreaming tools that did not take off. Clipping highlights out of long streams was the feature users actually wanted, so they rebuilt the company around it. The underlying pain is time: podcasters, streamers and YouTubers produce hours of long-form video, and every short-form platform rewards a steady stream of vertical clips. Cutting those clips by hand is slow, repetitive editing work.

OpusClip was founded in January 2022 by Young Zhao, Grace Wang and Jay Wu. The pivot to AI clipping came in 2023.

## How does OpusClip find the best moments in a long video?

A creator uploads a long video. Speech-to-text transcribes it, LLMs score moments for "virality", computer vision reframes the chosen moments to vertical, and automatic captions are added. The output is a set of ready-to-post shorts, ranked by how likely they are to perform.

Each step replaces a task an editor would otherwise do: finding the good moments, cropping for a phone screen, and subtitling.

After the pivot in 2023 the team launched a tool that finds the most engaging moments in a long video and cuts them into vertical shorts with captions. The stack combines several kinds of AI, speech recognition, language models and computer vision, into one pipeline. The product's value is the combination, not any single model. Team size is not disclosed with a dated primary source.

## How did OpusClip win creators and businesses?

Growth was product-led among podcasters and YouTubers, plus creator partnerships and a free tier that watermarks output. Every watermarked clip posted to a social platform carries the brand to new viewers, many of whom are creators themselves.

Pricing is freemium with monthly credit-based plans by minutes of video processed. That ties cost to usage, which matters because processing video with several models is expensive.

## What threatens an AI clipping tool like OpusClip?

The first challenge was the failure of the original livestreaming product. Many teams would have kept pushing the original plan; OpusClip treated the most-requested feature as a signal and rebuilt around it.

The second is the reliability of its headline number. The ~$20M ARR figure is labelled unverified because ARR Club repeats it from a newsletter we could not check ([unverified, ARR Club](https://www.arr.club/signal/opusclip-20m-arr-softbank-mar-2025)). Readers should treat it as a reported figure, not a confirmed one.

## How does OpusClip compare with Arcads and Suno?

[Arcads](/cases/arcads/) and OpusClip both sell AI video to marketers and creators, but from opposite inputs. OpusClip starts with a long video the customer already has and cuts it into clips. Arcads starts with only a script and generates the whole ad with an AI actor. OpusClip saves editing time; Arcads replaces filming.

[Suno](/cases/suno/) shows the consumer end of the same trend: AI that turns non-experts into makers. All three spread because their output is made to be posted, so every clip, ad or song is a demonstration. OpusClip's reported 10 million users and 57 billion views on its clips in a year show how far that loop can go.

## What lessons does OpusClip hold?

- **Pivot toward the job users repeat most.** Clipping long videos into shorts is a weekly task for creators.
- **Sell time saved, not technology.** Creators pay to skip hours of editing.
- **Output that gets posted is free marketing.** Billions of views on OpusClip clips spread the product.
- **Label shaky numbers honestly.** OpusClip's ARR figure is widely repeated but not confirmed by a primary source.

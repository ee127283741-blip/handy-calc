Clothes are usually designed and made months before anyone buys them. Taime Koe, CEO of Six Atomic in Singapore, says that for every retail dollar spent on clothing, about 70 cents is lost to discounts or thrown-away stock ([founder-reported, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/six-atomic)). His answer is to make clothes on demand, which only works if the slow, expert-heavy parts of production are automated. That is what Six Atomic builds.

## Why does fashion need on-demand production?

Mass production is good at economies of scale and cheap, plentiful clothing. It is bad at personalisation, sustainability and inventory risk. When a product is designed and produced months in advance, the chance it sells exactly as planned is low.

Making clothes only after they are sold fixes that, and opens the door to personalising design and fit for each buyer. Koe argues brands could then pay suppliers more and still earn more, which could even bring manufacturing closer to home. The obstacle is complexity: the supply chain needs many experts and vendors, so on-demand production needs a lot of automation.

## What does Six Atomic's software automate?

The pattern, the geometry used to cut fabric and sew a garment. If patterns can be created and adjusted automatically, any garment can fit any body and be exported in the format any factory uses. Six Atomic spent years studying pattern-making, from Victorian-era drafting texts to modern Japanese methods, then built algorithms that generate a unique pattern from a person's measurements.

| Product | What it does |
|---|---|
| Synthesis | Grades any design to any size in seconds, simulates the garment in 3D and outputs manufacturing-ready files |
| Inference | Calculates body measurements from simple inputs such as height and weight |
| Envision | Recommends the best size for a shopper and shows how each size fits their body |

Its clients range from enterprise brands such as Perry Ellis to small startups that want a flexible, personalisable supply chain.

## How did Six Atomic win enterprise clients first?

By becoming known as experts. The first enterprise clients came from speaking at conferences, industry referrals and plain hustle; later, op-eds in industry publications helped too. With a new client, the team starts with a proof of concept, then a scoping study with stakeholders, an R&D stage, development, deployment and ongoing support.

Starting at the top of the industry taught them how products are developed and how to build enterprise-grade architecture. They used that to shape turn-key SaaS products for smaller companies, sold through personalised cold email, search ads and SEO, though niche search volumes are low.

> "Bootstrap as long as you can." — Taime Koe ([founder-reported, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/six-atomic))

## What revenue does Six Atomic report?

Starter Story's headline and page header put Six Atomic at $40K a month in September 2022, but Koe does not state a revenue figure in the interview text, so this case labels it unverified ([unverified, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/six-atomic)). The company had two founders and 20 employees, and Koe said revenue kept growing while they bootstrapped. Total revenue is not disclosed.

The founders invested their own money at the start and have funded the business mainly through enterprise client projects. Koe expected enterprise income to remain most of the revenue for a few more years, with SaaS growing as smaller companies adopt the technology.

## How does Six Atomic compare with Launch Fast and Harvey?

[Launch Fast](/cases/launch-fast/), in the same e-commerce category, sells AI research tools to individual Amazon sellers and grew through a partner's audience. Six Atomic sells deep production technology to apparel brands and grew through conferences and referrals. One serves the small seller deciding what to sell; the other serves the brand deciding how to make it.

[Harvey](/cases/harvey/) shows the venture-funded version of selling AI to a demanding industry, with large rounds to fund long enterprise sales. Six Atomic took the opposite route, using enterprise projects themselves as funding. It is slower, but Koe values keeping control of the vision.

## What lessons does Six Atomic offer B2B founders?

- **Automate the bottleneck.** Patterns are the blueprint of every garment, so automating them unlocks on-demand production.
- **Learn from the biggest customers first.** Enterprise work shaped Six Atomic's later self-serve products.
- **Expertise is the sales channel.** Conferences, op-eds and referrals build the trust enterprise buyers need.
- **Client projects can fund a product.** Six Atomic bootstrapped on enterprise revenue instead of investors.

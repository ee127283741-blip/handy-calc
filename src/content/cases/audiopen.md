Louis Pereira works in his family's offline business during the day and builds on the internet at night. In March 2023 he built four tiny AI products in one week and hosted them on his personal website to avoid paying for domains. One of them struck a chord. That was AudioPen, and by February 2026 it was making between $15K and $20K a month ([founder-reported, Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/building-an-ai-tool-in-a-half-day-hackathon-and-hitting-20k-mo-BCloDTDRfjuip0pDghap)).

## Why did AudioPen stand out from four experiments?

Pereira calls himself a dabbler. He had started newsletters, built websites and web apps, and built apps for other people. When a new wave of AI APIs arrived in March 2023, he did what he always did: built small things fast and shared each one on Twitter. AudioPen got the strongest reaction, so he turned it into a standalone product.

AudioPen transcribes voice notes and transforms them into clear text. You talk through a messy idea and get back something readable. It is a simple promise that anyone who thinks faster than they type can understand.

## How do you build an AI app in 12 hours?

Pereira built AudioPen's first version at Half Day Build, a hackathon he organised where people tried to go from idea to revenue in 12 hours. The time limit forced him to build the simplest version anyone might pay for, and people did pay.

He is not a designer or a developer, but he can piece together no-code MVPs. He designed AudioPen in Figma and built it in Bubble, which costs about $30 a month ([founder-reported, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/audiopen)). He live-tweeted the whole build, sharing images and progress, which created hype and brought in his first beta testers, some of whom became paying customers. He has spent months refining the product since.

## How does a one-person AI tool grow with no ads?

Word of mouth has been the main channel since day one, helped by an affiliate program that rewards people for recommending it. Pereira spends nothing on ads or paid marketing, only on tools that improve the customer experience.

For retention he relies on email. He writes regular update emails with a personal touch, which makes users feel part of the product's journey and opens a direct line for feedback. He thinks update emails are underused, especially by young products.

> "The biggest takeaway I have is that life is random, so don’t get too emotionally attached to ideas in their infancy." — Louis Pereira ([founder-reported, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/audiopen))

## What does AudioPen AI earn a month?

AudioPen made about $15,000 a month in May 2024, a little over a year after launch ([founder-reported, Starter Story](https://www.starterstory.com/stories/audiopen)). By February 2026, Indie Hackers listed it at between $15K and $20K a month, about two years in ([founder-reported, Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/building-an-ai-tool-in-a-half-day-hackathon-and-hitting-20k-mo-BCloDTDRfjuip0pDghap)). Total revenue is not disclosed.

It has been profitable since day one because the costs are small. The biggest expense is the AI APIs, which Pereira says the business could not exist without.

## How does AudioPen compare with Talknotes and Magai?

[Talknotes](/cases/talknotes/) sells almost the same product, voice notes turned into structured content, and launched in the same wave. The difference is growth strategy. Talknotes' founder runs paid ads as his main channel and accepts a cost per customer. Pereira spends nothing on ads and relies on word of mouth and affiliates. Both reached profitability, which suggests the category is real and the channel is a matter of founder style.

[Magai](/cases/magai/) shares AudioPen's build path: a non-developer using Bubble to ship an AI product fast. Magai later had to rebuild on custom code as it grew; AudioPen has stayed small and simple enough for one part-time founder to run.

## What can makers learn from AudioPen?

- **Ship many small experiments.** Four products in a week found the one people wanted.
- **A time limit forces focus.** Twelve hours left room only for the core feature people would pay for.
- **Build in public during the build.** Live-tweeting turned onlookers into beta testers and customers.
- **Momentum is precious.** Pereira's second lesson is to grab it with both hands when it comes.
